Endorsing a first-time author is a small act with real impact. It's also easy to overthink. arXiv's guidance is actually quite specific about what endorsers should and shouldn't do, and knowing that makes the job lighter and fairer.

Endorse Commons is independent and not affiliated with arXiv. Endorsements are made on arXiv's own form, and arXiv's pages are the final word.

## What arXiv asks of you

arXiv's endorsement page sets out a short list.

### Know the person, or see the paper

arXiv says you **should know the person you endorse, or you should see the paper** that person intends to submit. On Endorse Commons you'll usually be in the second situation: the author shares their manuscript with you.

### Check it's appropriate for the subject area

This is the heart of it. arXiv says it doesn't expect you to read the paper in detail or verify the work is correct, **but you should check that the paper is appropriate for the subject area**.

Practically, that means asking:

- Does this paper belong in the category the author has chosen?
- Is it a genuine scholarly contribution of the kind that appears in this area?
- Does the author engage with relevant, current work?

### Know when not to endorse

arXiv says you **should not endorse** if:

- the author is **unfamiliar with the basic facts of the field**; or
- the work is **entirely disconnected from current work in the area**.

Neither of these is about whether you agree with the conclusions or would accept it at a top venue. The bar is "belongs in this community's conversation", not "is excellent".

### Treat the paper as confidential

arXiv says that if you're sent a paper to read, you should **treat it as privileged information and respect its confidentiality** as you would if asked to review it for a journal. See [Manuscript confidentiality for reviewers](/learn/manuscript-confidentiality).

### Record your decision on arXiv's form

After entering the author's code on arXiv's endorsement form, arXiv says you can tell it you **do or do not** wish to endorse the person. If you decide not to, arXiv asks you to use the form to say so explicitly; it's recorded as a negative endorsement. See [How to decline kindly](/learn/how-to-decline-kindly).

## What you're not responsible for

- **Peer review.** arXiv states that endorsement is not peer review.
- **Verifying correctness.** arXiv doesn't expect you to check proofs, reproduce experiments or confirm results.
- **Moderation.** Endorsed papers still go through arXiv's moderation, where volunteer subject experts check each submission. Your endorsement doesn't guarantee acceptance, and moderators may still reclassify or hold a paper.
- **The author's future conduct.** arXiv says the contents of submissions are the responsibility of the submitter, and it reserves the right to revoke endorsement from submitters who violate its policies.

## Why this matters more since 2026

As of January 2026, per arXiv, institutional email alone no longer qualifies new authors for automatic endorsement. arXiv made the change to stem a flood of low-quality, non-scientific submissions. That puts more weight on personal endorsers to apply arXiv's check thoughtfully, neither rubber-stamping nor gatekeeping beyond what arXiv asks.

## Fairness and conflicts

A few principles we encourage on Endorse Commons:

- **Judge the work, not the person's background.** Institution, country, career stage and English fluency aren't criteria arXiv lists.
- **Declare conflicts.** If you're a close collaborator or competitor, consider stepping back.
- **Never accept anything in exchange** for an endorsement.
- **Be kind.** A clear, respectful explanation helps an author far more than silence.

## Feedback is separate from endorsement

On Endorse Commons, many reviewers give feedback even when they decide not to endorse. That's valuable and entirely optional. See [Giving useful pre-submission feedback](/learn/giving-useful-feedback).
